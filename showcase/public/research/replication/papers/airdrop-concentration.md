# Airdrop Allocation Concentration: A Wallet-Level Field Test of the Tullock Active-Set Model

**Tsolmondorj Natsagdorj (0xSoftBoi)**
Suwappu Research
31 July 2026; materially revised 8 August 2026

*What this is: the empirical test our own theory paper required. We measure the HYPE genesis wallet allocation and the both-phase EIGEN Season 1 claim-recipient vector, with ENA Season 1 as a lower-resolution cross-check, and compare them with the model's matched-entrant active-set predictions. The sharp wallet-level prediction is rejected. The study does **not** identify beneficial owners or causally identify why the model fails. Budget constraints and capital-linked activity are hypotheses generated by the discrepancy, not estimates from these recipient ledgers. All material collection corrections remain in the released audit trail.*

*Suwappu operates a points program of the class analyzed here. Full disclosures in Section 8.*

---

## Executive summary

- **The active-set prediction is rejected at wallet level.** The model's median top-1 share is 17.1–40.8% over its original 5,000-entrant dispersion scenarios. At the observed HYPE and EIGEN wallet counts, the stored matched-*n* simulations span roughly **14.3–37.0%** across σ ∈ {0.2, 0.4, 1.0}. Relative to that lower edge, the measured top-1 share is **19.6× lower for HYPE** (0.73%; 90,912 recipient wallets) and **6.0× lower for raw EIGEN Season 1** (2.40%; 239,035 claim-recipient wallets). Observed positive-allocation wallet counts are orders of magnitude above the modeled active set.

- **The rejection is a grid-based simulation result, not a universal theorem.** At each program's entrant count, we evaluate 15 values of σ from 10⁻⁴ to 1.2 and 200 simulated economies per value. **Zero of 3,000 draws per program** jointly land within a factor of two of the observed top-1 share and at least half the observed wallet participation. We report the result for this prespecified model/grid; alternative behavioral models are not rejected by it.

- **Observed allocations remain highly concentrated.** The top 1% of wallets hold 58.6% of HYPE, 61.3% of raw EIGEN claims, and 62.7% of the measured ENA recipient vector. That concentration is economically relevant, but it has a much broader wallet-level shape than the model's tiny active set.

- **Wallets are not beneficial owners, and the bias direction is not generally one-way.** Splitting one entity across wallets can make person-level concentration higher than wallet-level measurement. Omnibus or custodial collection can make one wallet represent many beneficiaries and push in the opposite direction. Section 7 quantifies how much top-wallet clustering would be needed to restore the model's top-share prediction; it does not assume the answer.

- **ENA is a lower-resolution cross-check, not equal-quality primary evidence.** Four sibling claim contracts reconcile to approximately 750m seeded units. The corrected vector contains **46,198 wallets and 407.8m ENA** after excluding a **336.6m** issuer sweep-back. We do not compare that accounting directly with public "claimed percentage" figures unless their denominator, observation window, and sweep-back treatment are identical.

- **The causal explanation remains open.** Budget constraints, capital-linked activity, complete-information assumptions, and endogenous participation are plausible reasons the Tullock active-set channel fails. Recipient ledgers alone do not measure those mechanisms. The "capital mirror" interpretation is therefore a hypothesis to test prospectively, not a finding of this paper.

- **The collection-control result is independently useful.** Distributor-universe enumeration and seed = claims + sweeps + residuals checks caught the missing EIGEN Phase-2 distributor, an ENA sweep-back initially misclassified as a recipient, and a 47m ENA coverage gap. Those corrections are retained rather than overwritten.

## Why this matters

The theory paper told a founder to expect thin participation and told an allocator to underwrite a pro-rata points pool as roughly ten counterparties. The measured wallet populations do not support either statement. The replacement is **not** "there are hundreds of independent counterparties." The evidence only supports a wallet-level distribution: a broad positive-allocation population with roughly 59–63% of measured units in the top percentile of wallets.

For diligence, three questions must therefore remain separate: **allocation concentration** (measured here), **beneficial-owner concentration** (not measured here), and **post-distribution behavior** such as selling, coordination, or retention (also not measured here). A recipient ledger can answer the first; it cannot be promoted into the second or third without additional data.

## 1. The prediction under test

The **first edition** of the theory paper nominated recipient concentration at completed points-to-airdrop programs as its falsification variable and projected a highly concentrated active set. That paper has since been revised to record this test as a failed descriptive prediction while retaining the model as a conditional benchmark. The model outputs used here are unchanged; only their interpretation changed.

The quantitative bands (medians of 500 Monte Carlo draws, 5,000 potential entrants, lognormal cost dispersion σ): top-1 share 17.1% (σ=0.2) to 40.8% (σ=1.0); active participants 18 down to 5. The mechanism generating them is the asymmetric-Tullock active-set logic: with any material dispersion in marginal cost per point, the feasibility condition prices all but the cheapest few out of positive effort entirely.

One objection is disposed of before the data arrive: the original band was computed at 5,000 potential entrants, while the observed primary vectors contain 90,912 HYPE wallets and 239,035 EIGEN claim-recipient wallets. In the released matched-*n* simulations, HYPE's median top-1 share is 16.1% (σ=0.2) to 35.9% (σ=1.0), with the median active set falling from 20.5 to 6; EIGEN's is 14.35% to 36.98%, with the median active set falling from 22 to 6 (`concentration.json`, `model_band_matched_n`). Matching the observed wallet counts therefore does not resolve the quantitative gap; no stronger scale-invariance claim is needed.

Programs qualify for the comparison only when a materially pro-rata accumulation component can be identified: points scale with qualifying effort or capital and a fixed pool is allocated substantially by points share. Known nonlinear additions are not silently folded into the model comparison—EIGEN is shown both raw and with its documented per-address bonus removed under the stated approximation. Deliberately tiered or bespoke allocation formulas remain outside the model's scope.

## 2. Data

**HYPE genesis (Hyperliquid, 29 November 2024) — the anchor.** 31% of genesis supply was credited to point-holders pro rata, automatically, with no claim transaction required, and no exchange was distributing at genesis. A single free API call returns the complete 90,918-address genesis state. We exclude six system accounts, each individually identified and cross-checked against published tokenomics; the books then close exactly: user pool + undistributed genesis inside the emissions reserve + assistance fund + a $5k snapshot residual = 310,120,000.00 = the announced 310M genesis distribution plus the 0.012% HIP-2 allocation, to the cent. Remaining: **90,912 recipient wallets holding 269.6m HYPE.**

Honesty about what this vector is: it is a *post-eligibility* allocation, not the unobserved population before issuer rules were applied. Roughly 40.4m of the announced 310m did not reach the user vector and sits undistributed in the emissions reserve. Eligibility, anti-abuse, and/or jurisdictional rules can change both the numerator and denominator of concentration statistics. Because we do not observe the excluded allocation vector, **the direction and magnitude of that selection effect are not identified here**. The result therefore applies to the distributed wallet population actually observed, not to a hypothetical pre-filter contest population.

**EIGEN Season 1 (EigenLayer, claims 10 May – 7 September 2024).** Allocation was "proportional to Restaked Points" plus a flat 100-EIGEN bonus per qualifying user. Season 1 ran in **two phases with two distributor contracts**, and the first version of this collection missed the second: it scanned the Phase 1 distributor plus an Etherscan-labeled "Distributor 3" that emits zero transfers in the window, and shipped a Phase-1-only vector — a defect caught in adversarial review by the conservation check we now publish. The corrected collection sums every transfer out of both real distributors: **239,035 recipient wallets, 101.1m EIGEN**, with 22,974 wallets claiming in both phases (merged per wallet, the once-per-user bonus subtracted once per wallet, not per claim). The conservation identity closes exactly on both contracts: Phase 1 was seeded with 102,000,500 EIGEN and emitted 92,675,209 in claims plus a 9,325,291 post-deadline forfeiture sweep; Phase 2 was seeded with 10,632,000 and emitted its claims plus its own 2,225,807 sweep to the same forfeiture address in the same block — seeds equal claims plus sweeps to the cent, and the combined 112.6m seed matches the announced ~113m Season 1.

Claims introduce two unresolved selection channels. Never-claimed allocations are absent from the recipient vector, and jurisdictional eligibility restrictions can remove claimants of unknown size. Without the owed-but-unclaimed allocation vector, the net effect on top-wallet concentration is not identified. HYPE therefore carries more weight in the top-share rejection because it has no claim transaction, although its own pre-distribution eligibility filters remain a separate limitation. EIGEN still contributes strongly to the participation discrepancy because 239,035 positive-claim wallets are directly observed. The vector also contains a sub-100 cohort (including thousands of exactly-10.0 claims), so the flat-100 adjustment over-subtracts for that sub-population by at most ~0.5m on a ~71m adjusted pool; we retain the raw and adjusted views rather than hide the approximation.

**ENA Season 1 (Ethena, claims from 2 April 2024, vesting horizon scanned to November 2024) — the third program, measured after two collection corrections.** An earlier draft declared ENA unmeasurable; the distribution can instead be enumerated on-chain as **four sibling claim contracts, all seeded by the same Ethena funder within ~90 blocks, with seeds summing to approximately 750m**. Our first two collections then each contained an artifact the conservation check exposed: the funder's sweep-back sat in the vector as a false top recipient, and extending only one channel's scan left a 47m coverage hole. The corrected merge contains **46,198 recipient wallets and 407.8m ENA**, with **336.6m** swept back to the issuer. We do not state that this disproves any public "claimed percentage" statistic: such a comparison requires an identical denominator, time window, eligibility definition, and treatment of sweep-backs, which this paper has not established.

ENA enters the test as the lowest-resolution datapoint, with three limits stated rather than smoothed: recipients of record are claim executors, and some are plausibly custodial aggregators (the largest, at 21.9m ENA, may be one — wallet-level custody cannot be separated here); the enormous unclaimed mass makes claimed-versus-owed censoring worst of the three programs, in an unknown direction at the top; and outflow-based bookkeeping over-counts the seed identity by 11.5m (1.5%) via inter-channel hops, which affects the sweep/residual split but not the recipient vector, from which channel addresses are excluded. Superseded collections are retained in the release so both corrections are auditable.

## 3. Results

**Table 1. Measured concentration vs the model's predicted band**

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Recipients / active | 19–22 → 6 | 90,912 | 239,035 | 222,642 | 46,198 |
| Top-1 share | 14.3–16.4% → 35.9–37.0% | **0.73%** | **2.40%** | 3.11% | 5.36% |
| Top-10 share | 79.1–83.1% → 100% | 4.68% | 12.7% | 16.4% | 18.8% |
| Top-100 share | ≈ 100% | 21.4% | 32.4% | 42.0% | 44.7% |
| Top-1% share | ≈ 100% | 58.6% | 61.3% | 78.6% | 62.7% |
| Gini | — | 0.947 | 0.737 | 0.943 | 0.904 |
| Equal-holder equivalent (1/HHI) | — | 1,225 | 380 | 226 | 146 |

*Source: `data/airdrops/concentration.json`, computed by `code/analyze_airdrops.py` from the released recipient vectors. The model column uses that file's `model_band_matched_n` output at each program's observed wallet count; it is not the original n = 5,000 band in Paper 2. The equal-holder equivalent is the number of identical holders that would produce the observed HHI.*

Reading it plainly: the model predicted a tiny positive-effort set; the observed recipient vectors have a much broader positive-allocation population while remaining highly unequal. The top-10 wallets hold 4.7% of measured HYPE and 12.7% of raw EIGEN claims, against the model's roughly 78–100% top-10 band in the matched scenarios. The top percentile contains 909 HYPE wallets and 2,390 EIGEN wallets and holds roughly 60% of the measured units.

ENA, the lower-resolution third program, has top-1 5.4%, top-10 18.8%, top-1% 62.7%, and Gini 0.904 across 46,198 recipient wallets. Its larger top wallet may reflect an economic beneficiary, a claim executor, an omnibus address, or another aggregation mechanism; this dataset cannot distinguish them. The top-percentile share across the three measured vectors happens to span a narrow 58.6%–62.7% range. With three observations and unequal measurement quality, that is an empirical regularity to replicate, not a universal "shape."

The EIGEN bonus adjustment is an accounting decomposition, not a behavioral experiment. The additional 100-token per-address allocation is 22.9% of the measured raw claims; subtracting it under the stated adjustment rule moves Gini from 0.737 to 0.943 and the median claim from 112 tokens to 13.6. Paper 2 shows mechanically that a per-wallet bonus can create a splitting incentive under a fixed aggregate budget. **This dataset cannot estimate how many EIGEN wallets, if any, exist because of that incentive**, and the adjustment should not be read as causal evidence about sybil behavior.

The HYPE and bonus-adjusted EIGEN Ginis—0.947 and 0.943 under the baseline construction—motivate a narrower hypothesis: **recipient allocation may inherit the concentration of the capital or activity base used to earn points.** This paper does not observe the relevant pre-distribution capital vector, so it cannot test that mapping directly.

## 4. Where the model fails, and hypotheses for why

We asked the model the most sympathetic version of the question: at each program's own recipient count, what cost dispersion σ reproduces the observed top-1 share, and what participation does the model then imply?

**Table 2. The backed-out parameters**

| | Observed top-1 | σ required | Model's active set at that σ | Observed participation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| HYPE | 0.73% | 0.004 | ~808 | 90,912 |
| EIGEN | 2.40% | 0.019 | ~198 | 239,035 |
| ENA | 5.36% | 0.043 | ~82 | 46,198 |

*Source: `code/analyze_airdrops.py`, `backout_sigma` (25-draw medians, exact active-set solver from Paper 2, seed 20260731).*

No sampled σ matches both moments. For 15 values of σ log-spaced across 10⁻⁴–1.2, we draw 200 economies each at the program's entrant count and ask whether any draw jointly produces a top-1 share within a factor of two of the observed value and an active set of at least half the observed wallet count. **Zero of 3,000 draws per program hit** (`concentration.json`, `joint_rejection`; the simulation-based upper-tail resolution is <0.005 at each tested σ). The per-σ medians show the trade-off: near-zero dispersion raises modeled participation while collapsing the top share; increasing dispersion raises the top share while shrinking the active set. The observed pairs lie outside the region generated by this **tested grid and model**. This rejects the specified active-set model as a joint wallet-level description; it does not establish which alternative mechanism generated the data.

The participation mapping needs its own boundary. Inside the model, positive effort implies positive allocation. In the data, a positive allocation wallet is therefore the observable analogue we use for an active player, but it is **not behavioral proof that the wallet chose effort in response to an expected token prize**. Users may have undertaken the rewarded activity for other reasons; multiple wallets may share an owner; and one wallet may represent multiple beneficiaries. Setting potential entrants equal to observed positive recipients is a conservative matched-*n* construction for the model comparison, not an assertion that the real entrant population is known.

The discrepancy generates several hypotheses that require new data:

- **Binding budgets rather than unbounded effort.** Trading and restaking activities are naturally limited by participant capital. A budget-constrained pro-rata model can produce broad positive participation with a heavy upper tail, but this paper has not observed or estimated those budgets.
- **Lower dispersion in marginal cost than assumed.** The σ back-out required to match top-wallet concentration is small. That calculation is a model diagnostic, not a measurement of actual opportunity cost.
- **Activity that would occur without the prize.** If users trade, restake, or hold balances for primary product reasons and points are partly retroactive, positive allocations need not be equilibrium entry decisions into a contest.
- **Private information, risk, dynamics, and nonlinear costs.** Each relaxes assumptions that create the sharp complete-information active set and should be evaluated in alternative models.

What survives from the theory is correspondingly bounded. The **active-set concentration channel is rejected as a wallet-level description** for the primary measured programs. The fee-denomination identity and fixed-budget wallet-splitting invariance remain algebraically valid inside their assumptions, but this study does not validate their realized economic magnitude or prove live sybil behavior.

## 5. Decision implications

The paper changes three parts of our prior guidance and narrows a fourth.

- **Withdraw the "roughly ten counterparties" shortcut.** This study measures wallet concentration, not beneficial owners. Use the actual recipient vector for allocation concentration and a separately governed clustering process for counterparty/entity concentration.
- **Do not infer sell pressure from wallet shape alone.** Hundreds of large wallets could be independent, coordinated, custodial, or split identities. Unlock timing, transfer behavior, and realized selling require separate post-distribution data.
- **Treat mid-season concentration forecasting as a hypothesis.** If the capital-mirror explanation is right, pre-distribution balance/activity concentration should forecast the final allocation vector. That is testable out of sample; it has not been tested here.
- **Narrow the mechanism-design carryover.** The theory paper's original hard-cap claim has been retracted. Its fee-denomination result is conditional model arithmetic, and its wallet-splitting result applies to a fixed aggregate budget with no per-wallet nonlinearities. Neither result should be used as a blanket claim that a live program is sybil-neutral or distributionally broad.

For an institutional diligence process, the practical control set is therefore: reconcile the distributor population, version eligibility and claim filters, calculate wallet-level top-*k*/Gini/HHI, document the entity-resolution method separately, and avoid converting a ledger statistic into a behavioral or counterparty conclusion without additional evidence.

## 6. Prior work, and what is new here

The empirical airdrop literature measures adjacent quantities, and positioning against it sharpens what this paper adds.

The nearest antecedent, Messias, Yaish and Livshits ("Airdrops: Giving Money Away Is Harder Than It Seems," arXiv:2312.02752), studies major airdrops at recipient level but focuses on claim timing and post-claim disposition rather than the concentration test used here. Fritsch, Müller and Wattenhofer (arXiv:2204.01176) and Barbereau et al. (*Technology in Society* 73, 2023) measure governance-token concentration at post-distribution snapshots. Our vectors show that high wallet-level concentration can already be present at initial allocation; they do **not** estimate how much later trading adds to or subtracts from that concentration.

On the theory side, Georganas, Kiayias and Penna ("Airdrop Games," IJCAI-25) model identity splitting, and Luo et al. (arXiv:2503.14316) measure hunter profits on Hop and LayerZero. These papers reinforce why wallet identity is an economic variable rather than a person identifier. They do not imply a one-way measurement bias here: sybil splitting can reduce measured wallet concentration relative to entities, while custodial or omnibus addresses can aggregate many beneficiaries into one wallet.

Allen, Berg and Lane (*JBR* 163, 2023) discuss ownership decentralization as one rationale for airdrops. Our top-percentile wallet figures are relevant to that question but are **not** entity-level ownership measures, so we do not treat them as a direct decentralization score.

The contribution claimed here is deliberately specific: a reproducible recipient-vector concentration measurement, compared against a pre-existing quantitative active-set prediction, with correction history preserved. We do not rely on an exhaustive priority claim about the entire airdrop literature for the validity of the result.

## 7. What would change our view

- **More clean programs.** The primary inference rests most heavily on HYPE and EIGEN. Additional completed programs with reconstructable recipient populations and documented allocation rules can establish whether the result generalizes beyond this small sample. A clean pro-rata program with top-wallet concentration near the model band would reopen the active-set channel for that design class.
- **Beneficial-owner resolution.** Credible entity clustering is the most direct threat to the wallet-level rejection. Against the stored σ = 0.2 matched-*n* median, the top **63 HYPE wallets** would have to merge into one entity to reach 16.1%; the participation moment would require roughly 90,000 wallets to collapse to about 21 entities. EIGEN is more sensitive: merging its top **13 wallets** reaches its 14.35% matched median. These are wallet-merger illustrations, not entity-resolution results; omnibus addresses matter in the opposite direction and should be decomposed where possible.
- **Issuer-side allocation registries and filter logs.** For claims programs, an authoritative owed-allocation vector and explicit forfeiture/eligibility records would separate allocation design from claim behavior and reduce censoring ambiguity. It would also independently validate our on-chain distributor mapping for ENA.
- **A prospective capital/activity test.** The capital-mirror hypothesis predicts that pre-distribution balance or activity concentration should forecast allocation concentration. Joining those variables before the final allocation—and testing out of sample—would turn the current explanation from narrative into evidence.
- **Alternative behavioral models.** Explicit budget constraints, convex costs, private information, dynamic participation, or activity that would occur without rewards can be tested against the same two moments. A model that fits both participation and top-share concentration without post-hoc parameter freedom would supersede the rejected active-set specification.

## 8. Reproducibility

`code/collect_airdrops.py` collects all vectors from free public endpoints (hypurrscan for the HYPE genesis state; public Ethereum RPC logs for EIGEN and ENA), with checkpointed resumable log-scans, adaptive range-splitting for free-tier limits, and the seed/claims/sweep conservation checks that caught this paper's own collection errors. `code/ena_delta_merge.py` documents the recovery path of the final ENA merge. `code/analyze_airdrops.py` computes every statistic in Tables 1–2, including the σ back-out against the exact solver released with Paper 2, seed 20260731. Data: `hype_genesis_raw.json` (complete raw pre-exclusion snapshot), `hype_recipients.json`, `eigen_recipients.json` (both phases), `ena_recipients.json` (all four channels, funder sweep-back excluded and reported), the superseded collections (`ena_v1_partial_superseded.json` and the Phase-1-only EIGEN numbers preserved in the paper's own correction narrative), and `concentration.json`. The HYPE system-account exclusions are enumerated in the collector with their reconciliation against published tokenomics, so the single most judgment-laden step in the pipeline is inspectable.

## 9. Disclosures

This is research, not investment, legal, accounting, or prudential advice. Suwappu has a commercial interest in fee-denominated incentive design. This paper rejects one more dramatic claim from our own theory paper; the current revision of that theory paper also narrows its hard-cap and wallet-splitting claims. We hold no position in HYPE, EIGEN, or ENA taken on the basis of this analysis. No issuer named here reviewed this work. The primary evidence is public chain state or public APIs collected 31 July–1 August 2026. All concentration statistics are wallet-level. The review process found a missing EIGEN Phase-2 distributor, an overinterpreted Gini coincidence, HYPE eligibility-filter ambiguity, and two ENA collection defects; the corrected inputs and superseded artifacts are retained in the replication bundle.
