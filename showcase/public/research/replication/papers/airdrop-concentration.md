# Who Actually Collected the Airdrops: Testing the Tullock Active-Set Prediction Against Completed Allocations

**Tsolmondorj Natsagdorj (0xSoftBoi)**
Suwappu Research
31 July 2026; revised 1 August 2026 after adversarial refereeing

*What this is: the empirical test our own theory paper said should be run against it. "Points Programs as Tullock Contests" (Suwappu Research, July 2026) predicted that a continuous pro-rata points pool is captured by a handful of operators — a median top-1 share of 17–41% of the pool and 5–18 active participants out of thousands — and named the public variable that would falsify it: recipient-level concentration at completed programs. We collected complete recipient-level data for three such programs from free public endpoints: Hyperliquid's HYPE genesis (allocation), EigenLayer's EIGEN Season 1 (claims, both phases), and Ethena's ENA Season 1 (claims, all four distribution channels). The sharp prediction fails in all three, by one to two orders of magnitude, in both directions at once. This paper reports the failure, measures what the data actually look like — the top percentile of wallets holds 59–63% in every program — and identifies which of the model's assumptions broke. Every collection error our adversarial referee process caught, including two of our own, is documented in the release.*

*Suwappu operates a points program of the class analyzed here. Full disclosures in Section 8.*

---

## Executive summary

- **The active-set prediction is rejected at wallet level, jointly and decisively.** The model's median top-1 recipient share across its assumed dispersion range is 17.1–40.8% of the pool (14.7–35.9% recomputed at the programs' own entrant counts). Measured: **0.73%** (HYPE genesis, 90,912 recipients, post-enforcement allocation) and **2.40%** (EIGEN Season 1 both phases, 239,035 recipients, claimed). The model's active set is 5–23 wallets; the measured participation is four orders of magnitude wider.

- **No parameterization of the model rescues it, and the rejection is formal.** Backing out the cost dispersion σ that would reproduce the observed top-1 share, at each program's own entrant count, gives σ = 0.004 (HYPE) and σ = 0.019 (EIGEN) — economically zero heterogeneity — at which the model still predicts only ~800 and ~200 active participants. A sup-over-σ Monte Carlo test makes it exact: across 15 dispersion values spanning σ = 10⁻⁴ to 1.2, in 3,000 draws per program, **not one draw produces simultaneously a top-1 share within a factor of two of the observed one and an active set of even half the observed participation** (sup-p < 0.005, both programs). Even at σ = 10⁻⁴ — cost differences of a hundredth of a percent — the active-set filter admits only ~18,000–21,000 of the potential entrants while driving top-1 to 0.03%. The two observed moments lie off the model's attainable frontier at every parameter value: the failure is structural, not a calibration miss.

- **Concentration is nonetheless severe — it just has the wrong shape for a contest and the right shape for a wealth distribution.** The top 1% of recipient wallets hold 58.6% (HYPE) and 61.3% (EIGEN) of their pools; allocation Ginis are 0.947 and 0.738 raw. Strip EIGEN's flat 100-token per-user bonus to isolate the pro-rata component and its Gini rises to 0.943, against HYPE's 0.947. We deliberately do not lean on how close those two numbers are — trimming choices move either by several hundredths, and an earlier draft's "identical to three decimals" claim died in refereeing when the corrected two-phase EIGEN vector shifted it — the robust statement is that both allocations sit in the 0.88–0.95 band across every reasonable trim, which is where studies of on-chain wealth distributions sit. Two programs is suggestion, not proof, but the suggestion is pointed: pro-rata allocations mirror the capital distribution of the participating population, not the equilibrium of a rent-seeking contest.

- **Both numbers are lower bounds on person-level concentration.** Wallet-level data cannot merge sybils; a farmer splitting across wallets appears as many small recipients. The rejection of the *sharp* prediction survives this caveat — rescuing the model's top-1 band requires HYPE's wealthiest 55 wallets to be a single entity, and rescuing its participation prediction requires ~90,000 wallets to collapse into ~21 people — but every share reported here should be read as a floor, and Section 7 quantifies exactly how much clustering would overturn which conclusion.

- **The third program replicates the pattern at lower resolution — and contradicts an industry claim on the way.** Ethena's ENA Season 1, which an earlier draft called unmeasurable, enumerates exactly on-chain: four sibling claim contracts, all seeded by the same Ethena address, summing to the announced 750M to within dust. Merging all four over the full claim-plus-vesting horizon: **46,198 recipient wallets claimed 407.8m ENA — and 336.6m, 45% of the pool, was swept back to the issuer unclaimed**, against a widely-repeated "96% claimed" figure that does not survive the ledger. Among actual recipients: top-1 5.4%, top-1% **62.7%**, Gini 0.904 — the same shape as the other two programs, measured with three stated limits (claim executors may be custodial; the huge unclaimed mass makes claimed-vs-owed censoring worst here; and outflow bookkeeping over-counts by 11.5m, 1.5% of seed, via inter-channel hops).

- **Measurement discipline generalizes, and we publish our own violations of it.** The distributor universe must be enumerated and conservation-checked (seed = claims + sweeps + residuals) before any concentration number is real: that check caught our missing EIGEN Phase-2 distributor, the ENA funder's 336.6m sweep-back masquerading as a top recipient, and a 47m coverage hole — each documented in the release. It is the same universe-enumeration discipline our USDT0 work learned on the liability side, wearing a different asset.

## Why this matters

Two of our own published claims are at stake. The theory paper told a founder to "expect thin participation dominated by a handful of professional operators" and told an allocator that a pro-rata program "should be underwritten as an allocation to roughly ten counterparties." On the evidence below, the first claim is wrong at wallet level for the two largest clean programs we can measure, and the second is wrong as stated: the correct underwriting unit is not ten counterparties but a top-percentile cohort — hundreds to a couple of thousand wallets holding roughly 60% — whose composition looks like the capital distribution of the program's user base.

The distinction matters for diligence. "Ten wallets own the pool" and "the top 1% own 60% of the pool" imply different unlock behavior, different sell-pressure dynamics, and different sybil-filter payoffs. The first is a counterparty problem; the second is a distribution shape, and it appears to be *the same shape* across programs. If that regularity holds beyond two observations, the diligence question stops being "did farming concentrate this program?" and becomes "what is the wealth distribution of the population this program recruited?" — which is knowable *before* the program runs.

## 1. The prediction under test

The theory paper's falsification criterion, quoted from its own Section 8: *"For completed points-to-airdrop programs with public allocation snapshots, compute the top-10 share of allocated supply. The model predicts it lands near the Table 2 band, roughly 25% to 66% for the top recipient alone at wide dispersion. A measured top-10 share in the low single digits across several programs falsifies the mechanism as the dominant force."*

The quantitative bands (medians of 500 Monte Carlo draws, 5,000 potential entrants, lognormal cost dispersion σ): top-1 share 17.1% (σ=0.2) to 40.8% (σ=1.0); active participants 18 down to 5. The mechanism generating them is the asymmetric-Tullock active-set logic: with any material dispersion in marginal cost per point, the feasibility condition prices all but the cheapest few out of positive effort entirely.

One objection is disposed of before the data arrive: the band was computed at 5,000 potential entrants, and our programs have 90,913 and 218,014. Re-running the model at each program's own entrant count barely moves it — at n = 90,913, median top-1 is 16.1% (σ=0.2) to 35.9% (σ=1.0) with an active set of 20 down to 6; at n = 218,014, 14.7% to 35.0% with 23 down to 6 (`concentration.json`, `model_band_matched_n`). The active-set logic is effectively scale-free in the entrant count — more potential entrants are simply priced out — so nothing below can be attributed to comparing against the wrong n.

Programs qualify for the test only if their mechanism is continuous pro-rata accumulation — points proportional to effort or capital, fixed pool split by points share, no caps, tiers, or discretionary buckets. Deliberately tiered designs (Jito, Jupiter, zkSync's capped formula, LayerZero's bespoke scoring) are outside the model's scope and outside this test.

## 2. Data

**HYPE genesis (Hyperliquid, 29 November 2024) — the anchor.** 31% of genesis supply was credited to point-holders pro rata, automatically, with no claim transaction required, and no exchange was distributing at genesis. A single free API call returns the complete 90,918-address genesis state. We exclude six system accounts, each individually identified and cross-checked against published tokenomics; the books then close exactly: user pool + undistributed genesis inside the emissions reserve + assistance fund + a $5k snapshot residual = 310,120,000.00 = the announced 310M genesis distribution plus the 0.012% HIP-2 allocation, to the cent. Remaining: **90,912 recipient wallets holding 269.6m HYPE.**

Honesty about what this vector is: it is the *post-enforcement* allocation, not a raw contest outcome. Roughly 40.4m of the announced 310m — 13% — never reached users and sits undistributed in the emissions reserve, and the 90,918-address state compares to a publicized ~94,000 eligible figure: an eligibility, sybil/wash-trading and jurisdiction filter demonstrably operated before genesis. An earlier draft called this vector "not a claims-filtered shadow"; that contrast was overstated, and we withdraw it. What survives is the direction of the bias: pre-genesis filtering prunes clustered and penalized wallets, which *raises* measured concentration — toward the model we reject — and no filter produces a smooth 90,912-wallet power law by removing a 17–41% single winner plus an entire five-to-twenty-wallet capture cohort. The rejection does not depend on the filtered mass.

**EIGEN Season 1 (EigenLayer, claims 10 May – 7 September 2024).** Allocation was "proportional to Restaked Points" plus a flat 100-EIGEN bonus per qualifying user. Season 1 ran in **two phases with two distributor contracts**, and the first version of this collection missed the second: it scanned the Phase 1 distributor plus an Etherscan-labeled "Distributor 3" that emits zero transfers in the window, and shipped a Phase-1-only vector — a defect caught in adversarial review by the conservation check we now publish. The corrected collection sums every transfer out of both real distributors: **239,035 recipient wallets, 101.1m EIGEN**, with 22,974 wallets claiming in both phases (merged per wallet, the once-per-user bonus subtracted once per wallet, not per claim). The conservation identity closes exactly on both contracts: Phase 1 was seeded with 102,000,500 EIGEN and emitted 92,675,209 in claims plus a 9,325,291 post-deadline forfeiture sweep; Phase 2 was seeded with 10,632,000 and emitted its claims plus its own 2,225,807 sweep to the same forfeiture address in the same block — seeds equal claims plus sweeps to the cent, and the combined 112.6m seed matches the announced ~113m Season 1.

Two bias channels, stated in both directions. Claims data omit never-claimed allocations, which are disproportionately small — biasing measured concentration *up*, toward the model we reject. But EIGEN's claims also barred several jurisdictions outright, and a barred entity's unclaimed allocation can be arbitrarily large: with 11.6m forfeited, a single barred claimant at the model's band-edge share is not arithmetically excluded. We therefore lean the top-1 rejection on HYPE (no claim step, immune to claim-censoring) and rest EIGEN's contribution on its participation moment, which censoring can only understate. The vector also contains a sub-100 cohort (thousands of exactly-10.0 claims), so a no-bonus floor sub-population exists; the flat-100 adjustment over-subtracts for it by at most ~0.5m on a ~71m pool, and we state it rather than smooth it.

**ENA Season 1 (Ethena, claims from 2 April 2024, vesting horizon scanned to November 2024) — the third program, measured after we first got it wrong twice.** An earlier draft declared ENA unmeasurable, citing custodial channels and an unrecoverable distributor set; adversarial review showed both claims were wrong in the details: the distribution enumerates exactly on-chain as **four sibling claim contracts, all seeded by the same Ethena funder within ~90 blocks, seeds summing to the announced 750m to within dust** — no issuer registry needed. Our own first two collections then each contained an artifact the conservation check exposed: the funder's sweep-back of unclaimed funds sat in the vector as a fake 40%+ "top recipient," and extending only one channel's scan left a 47m coverage hole. The corrected merge over all four channels and the full horizon: **46,198 recipient wallets, 407.8m ENA claimed — with 336.6m (45%) swept back to the issuer unclaimed**. The widely-cited "96.3% claimed" figure for this program does not survive its own ledger.

ENA enters the test as the lowest-resolution datapoint, with three limits stated rather than smoothed: recipients of record are claim executors, and some are plausibly custodial aggregators (the largest, at 21.9m ENA, may be one — wallet-level custody cannot be separated here); the enormous unclaimed mass makes claimed-versus-owed censoring worst of the three programs, in an unknown direction at the top; and outflow-based bookkeeping over-counts the seed identity by 11.5m (1.5%) via inter-channel hops, which affects the sweep/residual split but not the recipient vector, from which channel addresses are excluded. Superseded collections are retained in the release so both corrections are auditable.

## 3. Results

**Table 1. Measured concentration vs the model's predicted band**

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Recipients / active | 23 → 5 | 90,912 | 239,035 | 222,642 | 46,198 |

| Top-10 share | ~78% → 100% | 4.68% | 12.7% | 16.4% | 18.8% |
| Top-100 share | ≈ 100% | 21.4% | 32.4% | 42.0% | 44.7% |
| Top-1% share | ≈ 100% | 58.6% | 61.3% | 78.6% | 62.7% |
| Gini | — | 0.947 | 0.738 | 0.943 | 0.904 |

*Source: `data/airdrops/concentration.json`, computed by `code/analyze_airdrops.py` from `hype_recipients.json` and `eigen_recipients.json`. Model band: `data/tullock_mc.json` (Paper 2, Table 2). The equal-holder equivalent is the number of identical holders that would produce the observed HHI.*

Reading it plainly: the model predicted a pool owned by a handful; the data show pools owned by a *heavy-tailed crowd*. The top-10 wallets — the variable the theory paper nominated — hold 4.7% and 12.7%, against a predicted ~78–100%. The distribution's business end is not the top ten but the top percentile: 909 wallets (HYPE) and 2,390 wallets (EIGEN) holding ~60%.

ENA, the low-resolution third program, lands on the same shape: top-1 5.4% (against the matched-n band's 15%+ bottom edge), top-10 18.8%, top-1% 62.7%, Gini 0.904 across 46,198 claiming wallets. Its higher top-1 than the other two programs is exactly what its limits predict — claim executors can aggregate custodially, and its largest recipient (21.9m) is plausibly one — and even so it sits at a third of the band's bottom edge. The top-percentile share across all three programs spans 58.6% to 62.7%: three chains, three activities, three formulas, one shape.

The EIGEN bonus adjustment shows design floors doing exactly what the theory paper's sybil analysis said they do, in the aggregate: the flat 100-token bonus is 22.9% of all claimed tokens, and removing it moves the Gini from 0.738 to 0.943 and the median claim from 112 tokens to 13.6. The floor compresses measured inequality without changing who holds the top of the distribution — and its per-user structure is, per Paper 2's corollary, a sybil incentive, so some unknown fraction of the 239,035 wallets exists *because* of the floor. Both effects push measured concentration down; neither rescues the active-set prediction, which fails in the direction the biases cannot explain.

The two Ginis — 0.947 and 0.943 at baseline, 0.88–0.95 under any reasonable trim — deserve one restrained sentence rather than a coincidence story: across different chains, different activities (trading vs restaking), and different formulas, both allocations land in the band where studies of on-chain wealth distributions sit, which is consistent with the simplest reading of everything above: **a pro-rata program photographs the capital distribution of its participants.**

## 4. Why the model failed: the bridge, then the diagnosis

We asked the model the most sympathetic version of the question: at each program's own recipient count, what cost dispersion σ reproduces the observed top-1 share, and what participation does the model then imply?

**Table 2. The backed-out parameters**

| | Observed top-1 | σ required | Model's active set at that σ | Observed participation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| HYPE | 0.73% | 0.004 | ~808 | 90,912 |
| EIGEN | 2.40% | 0.019 | ~198 | 239,035 |
| ENA | 5.36% | 0.043 | ~82 | 46,198 |

*Source: `code/analyze_airdrops.py`, `backout_sigma` (25-draw medians, exact active-set solver from Paper 2, seed 20260731).*

No σ matches both moments, and the sup-over-σ test formalizes it. For 15 values of σ log-spaced across 10⁻⁴–1.2, we drew 200 economies each at the program's own entrant count and asked whether any draw jointly produced a top-1 share within a factor of two of the observed one *and* an active set of at least half the observed participation — windows generous enough that a model in the right neighborhood would hit them constantly. **Zero of 3,000 draws hit, for any of the three programs** (`concentration.json`, `joint_rejection`; sup-p < 0.005). The per-σ medians show why: the two moments trade off monotonically. At σ = 10⁻⁴, participation rises only to ~18,000–21,000 while top-1 collapses to 0.03%; by σ = 0.011, top-1 reaches 1.7% but participation has fallen to ~300. The observed pairs — (0.73%, 90,913) and (2.62%, 218,014) — are unreachable from any point of the parameter space. Matching the top means assuming participants' marginal costs are identical to within half a percent; even then the model excludes the overwhelming majority of observed participants. The active-set channel — dispersion prices out the crowd — is simply not what generated these allocations.

One framing point, for fairness in both directions. Comparing the model's active set against the count of positive-allocation wallets is exact, not approximate: in a pro-rata contest, zero effort means zero points means zero allocation, so positive-allocation recipients are the empirical counterpart of the model's active players, and setting the model's potential-entrant pool equal to that count is the maximally sympathetic choice — any larger true pool only widens the participation gap, since the active set barely grows in n. And because points at both programs predated any announced token (Hyperliquid's by design; EigenLayer's by roughly ten months), the test is properly a joint test of the contest model *plus* rational prize expectations — professional farmers demonstrably acted on expected prizes, so the expectations half is not vacuous, but a reader should carry the joint framing.

The diagnosis was partly anticipated and partly not. The theory paper's limitations section named convex costs and risk aversion as reasons real concentration should be milder than modelled; it did not anticipate the binding constraint the data reveal — bounded budgets with near-uniform marginal costs, the exact inversion of its unbounded-effort, heterogeneous-cost setup. The data pick the mechanisms out cleanly:

- **Points in these programs are denominated in capital, and capital's *marginal cost* is nearly uniform while its *endowment* is wildly unequal.** A point earned by restaked ETH or by margin-backed volume costs roughly the same opportunity spread for a whale as for a retail wallet — σ ≈ 0, exactly what the back-out finds. What differs across participants by orders of magnitude is the *budget*. The model has unbounded effort and heterogeneous marginal cost; reality had bounded budgets and homogeneous marginal cost. Under budget-bound pro-rata, allocation ∝ capital deployed, and the allocation distribution is the capital distribution — Gini 0.95, heavy tail, no tiny active set. That is what both programs show.
- **Participation was not an equilibrium best response.** 90,913 and 218,014 wallets did not compute entry conditions; they used products and got paid for it retroactively (Hyperliquid's points preceded any announced conversion). The complete-information Nash filter — the step that generates the tiny active set — never operated.

What this rescues, and what it kills, in the theory paper: the **denomination and sybil-neutrality results survive untouched** — they are mechanism arithmetic, independent of who shows up (and EIGEN's floor demonstrates the per-wallet-bonus incentive at scale). The **active-set concentration story dies as a description of program-wide allocation** and survives only, if anywhere, on the professional-farming margin *within* the crowd — a margin wallet-level data cannot isolate, and which we decline to claim without being able to measure it.

## 5. What this changes in our published advice

The theory paper's design guidance (denominate in fees, no per-wallet floors, caps don't reduce dissipation) stands; its underwriting guidance changes:

- Was: "underwrite as an allocation to roughly ten counterparties." Now: **underwrite the top percentile of wallets as holding ~60%, and model its composition as the capital distribution of the recruited population.** For unlock and sell-pressure analysis, hundreds-to-thousands of uncoordinated large holders behave differently from ten coordinated operators — less cliff risk, more persistent drift.
- New, and testable in advance: because allocation mirrors participant capital, **a program's eventual concentration is forecastable from its depositor distribution mid-season** — no waiting for the airdrop. That is a monitorable statistic, and we intend to publish it for live programs.
- The sybil question inverts. If allocation ∝ capital and splitting capital across wallets changes nothing (fee/capital-proportional points are sybil-neutral by the theory paper's own corollary), then wallet-count-based sybil filters on pro-rata programs mostly prune the *bottom* of the distribution — cosmetic for concentration, real for headcount. Filters earn their keep only against floor/bonus exploits, which are a design choice.

## 6. Prior work, and what is new here

The empirical airdrop literature measures adjacent quantities, and positioning against it sharpens what this paper adds.

The nearest antecedent, Messias, Yaish and Livshits ("Airdrops: Giving Money Away Is Harder Than It Seems," arXiv:2312.02752), studies nine major airdrops at recipient level — but measures claim timing and post-claim *dumping* (up to 66% of tokens sold, often in the recipient's first transaction), not allocation-time concentration. Fritsch, Müller and Wattenhofer (arXiv:2204.01176) and Barbereau et al. (*Technology in Society* 73, 2023) measure governance-token concentration with the same Gini/top-k toolkit we use — finding holder Ginis near 0.99 for UNI and COMP — but at *post-trading* snapshots, where concentration confounds initial allocation with subsequent accumulation. Our allocation-time Gini of ~0.95 therefore adds the missing decomposition: the concentration those studies observe is close to fully present at distribution, before any secondary-market trading. Industry measurement (Keyrock's 62-airdrop study; Nansen's ARB design retrospectives, notable because Nansen *built* a deliberately capped allocation and then watched recipient activity collapse ~90% post-claim) documents outcomes and retention, again without recipient-level allocation distributions against a stated model.

On the theory side, Georganas, Kiayias and Penna ("Airdrop Games," IJCAI-25) model airdrop participation with sybil identity-splitting in equilibrium — a formal reason to read every wallet-level statistic here as a lower bound — and Luo et al. (arXiv:2503.14316) measure hunter profits on Hop and LayerZero, the expenditure side of the contest whose allocation side we measure. The contest-empirics genre itself (Dechenaux, Kovenock and Sheremeta's survey of experimental Tullock tests; the Oklahoma land-rush studies and Del Rosal's survey of field dissipation measurements) is thick on laboratory dissipation and thin on field tests of *concentration* predictions — Del Rosal documents how rarely credible field measurements exist at all, which is the gap a completed airdrop's public ledger happens to fill for free.

Two findings here also bear on the framing literature: Allen, Berg and Lane (*JBR* 163, 2023) catalogue "decentralization of ownership" as a primary airdrop rationale, and a measured top-1% share of ~60% at allocation time directly qualifies it; and independent industry tallies of the HYPE genesis (PANews/ChainCatcher: ~94,000 wallets, average ≈2,915 HYPE) corroborate our per-wallet figures from a source we do not control.

To our knowledge — and we searched for it, since it would have saved us the work — no prior study computes recipient-level allocation concentration for completed programs and tests it against a rent-seeking contest model's quantitative predictions. That comparison, and its failure, is the contribution.

## 7. What would change our view

- **More programs.** Two clean observations. Kamino S1 (points-linear, Solana merkle distributor) is the natural third; any program with a published, complete, non-custodial distributor registry qualifies. A clean pro-rata program showing a genuine 25%+ top-1 wallet share would put the active-set channel back in play.
- **Person-level resolution.** Any credible wallet-clustering of these recipient sets. The arithmetic of what a rescue requires, computed from the actual vectors: for HYPE's top-1 share to reach even the bottom of the matched-n band, the **top 55 wallets** would all have to be one entity (the top 358 for the band's top); for the participation moment, 90,913 wallets would have to collapse to ~21 entities. EIGEN is the softer case — merging only its **top 12–16 wallets** reaches the band's bottom edge, and a single fund claiming through a dozen addresses is a documented real-world pattern — so we lean the top-1 rejection on HYPE and rest EIGEN's contribution on its participation moment, which censoring and clustering can only strengthen (218,014 observed wallets would need to be ~23 entities). If clustering evidence ever shows the *wealthiest* wallets of these programs to be single entities at that scale, the model's prediction was right and our wallet-level rejection wrong — the single most damaging possibility for this paper, flagged rather than buried. Two structural forces cut the other way: both issuers ran pre-distribution sybil filtering that pruned clustered wallets, and under pure pro-rata the theory paper's own corollary removes any strategic gain from splitting.
- **An issuer-published distributor registry for ENA** (or any program we called unmeasurable), which would convert the Section 2 refusal into a number.
- **Evidence of effort-denominated programs behaving differently.** The capital-mirror hypothesis predicts that programs whose points cost *effort with genuinely dispersed unit costs* (labor, infrastructure) — not capital — should concentrate more like the model. A measured example either way would sharpen the domain boundary.

## 8. Reproducibility

`code/collect_airdrops.py` collects all vectors from free public endpoints (hypurrscan for the HYPE genesis state; public Ethereum RPC logs for EIGEN and ENA), with checkpointed resumable log-scans, adaptive range-splitting for free-tier limits, and the seed/claims/sweep conservation checks that caught this paper's own collection errors. `code/ena_delta_merge.py` documents the recovery path of the final ENA merge. `code/analyze_airdrops.py` computes every statistic in Tables 1–2, including the σ back-out against the exact solver released with Paper 2, seed 20260731. Data: `hype_genesis_raw.json` (complete raw pre-exclusion snapshot), `hype_recipients.json`, `eigen_recipients.json` (both phases), `ena_recipients.json` (all four channels, funder sweep-back excluded and reported), the superseded collections (`ena_v1_partial_superseded.json` and the Phase-1-only EIGEN numbers preserved in the paper's own correction narrative), and `concentration.json`. The HYPE system-account exclusions are enumerated in the collector with their reconciliation against published tokenomics, so the single most judgment-laden step in the pipeline is inspectable.

## 9. Disclosures

This is research, not investment advice. Suwappu operates a fee-denominated points program of the class analyzed in the theory paper; this paper's results *weaken* one published claim about such programs (extreme active-set concentration) and leave the design results our program relies on unchanged — readers should notice that this correction runs against the more dramatic version of our own prior work, and judge the incentive accordingly. We hold no position in HYPE, EIGEN, or ENA taken on the basis of this analysis. No issuer named here reviewed this work. All data is public chain state or public APIs, collected 31 July – 1 August 2026. This paper was adversarially refereed before release; the referee pass found the missing EIGEN Phase-2 distributor, the fragile Gini-identity claim, and the undisclosed enforcement filters now documented in Section 2, and we incorporated its surviving findings rather than argued with them.
